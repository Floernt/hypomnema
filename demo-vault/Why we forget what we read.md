# Why we forget what we read

Most readers know the feeling. You finish a book you enjoyed, you close it with the sense of having learned something, and three weeks later you could not summarise its argument in two sentences. The book did not fail you. The way you read it did.

## The forgetting curve

In 1885, Hermann Ebbinghaus measured how fast he forgot lists of nonsense syllables. %%[a1%%The curve he drew drops steeply in the first hours, then flattens%%a1]%%: most of what is lost is lost almost immediately. Later research has refined the shape of the curve, but not its lesson. Memory is not a recording that slowly fades; it is a reconstruction that has to be rebuilt each time we call on it.

Passive reading feeds this curve. The eyes move across the page, the sentences make sense one after another, and the feeling of understanding is real. But ==understanding a sentence is not the same as retaining an idea==. Retention requires the reader to do something with what they read.

## Reading with a pen

For centuries, serious readers wrote in their books. Medieval scholars filled the margins of manuscripts with glosses, objections and cross-references. Montaigne annotated his Latin authors before turning those notes into essays. %%[a2%%Fermat famously claimed that a margin was too narrow to contain his proof%%a2]%%, and mathematicians spent three centuries looking for it.

These marginalia were not decoration. %%[a3%%They were the trace of a dialogue between the reader and the text%%a3]%%: agreement, doubt, a link to another book, a question to come back to. The act of writing forces a choice — what matters here, and why — and that choice is precisely what memory keeps.

## What changes with digital notes

Digital reading made annotation easier and, paradoxically, weaker. Highlighting a passage takes a fraction of a second, so we highlight too much and think too little. The highlight records *where* we were struck, not *what* we thought.

The useful move is to separate the two layers. The source text stays intact; your thinking lives next to it, %%[a4%%%%a4]%%visibly distinct, dated, and connected to the rest of your notes. Comments become searchable, categorised, and able to answer each other when you come back to the text months later.

## A modest method

Read with a question in mind. When a passage strikes you, write one sentence about why. When you disagree, say so. When an idea connects to something you already know, name the link. Then, a week later, read only your comments. If they still make sense without the text, the reading has done its work.
